## Q1. What is the definition of an AI system under the AI Act?
*Category: Definition · Expected:  · Time: 4.8 s · Best relevance: 7.29*

**Answer:** An “AI system” under the AI Act is a machine‑based system designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy, which may become adaptive after deployment and, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from its inputs how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments[4].  

**Key points**

- The definition is set out in Article 3(1) of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the AI Act, and is binding law.  
- The Commission’s non‑binding guidelines (sources [1] and [3]) refer to this definition to help providers determine whether their technology falls within the scope of the Act, but they do not change the legal wording.  

Thus, any system that meets the description above is considered an AI system for the purposes of the AI Act.

## Q2. Can a company use AI for social scoring of people?
*Category: Prohibition · Expected:  · Time: 3.2 s · Best relevance: 4.60*

No. The EU AI Act bans AI‑enabled “social scoring” of natural persons or groups when the score leads to detrimental or unfavourable treatment in contexts unrelated to the data’s original purpose.

**Key points**

* **Binding rule** – Article 5(1)(c) of the AI Act (as amended by Regulation 2024/1689) expressly prohibits the placing on the market, putting into service or use of AI systems that evaluate or classify people based on their social behaviour or inferred personal characteristics if the resulting score causes detrimental or unfavourable treatment in unrelated social contexts[4].  
* **Scope** – The prohibition applies to both public and private actors and covers any AI system that produces a “social score” used for decisions such as housing, employment, insurance, etc.[1][3].  
* **Guidance** – The Commission’s non‑binding guidelines explain that such practices are considered unacceptable social scoring and are prohibited regardless of whether the AI system is provided or used by a public or private person[1]. They also note that the rule only applies to scoring of natural persons, not legal entities, unless the score is based on personal characteristics[2].  

Thus, a company may not legally use AI to produce social scores that affect people’s treatment in the manner described by the Act. (The guidance is interpretative and non‑binding, but the law itself creates the obligation.)

**Sources:**
- [1] Commission Guidelines on prohibited AI practices, page 62 (Non-binding guidance)
- [2] Commission Guidelines on prohibited AI practices, page 63 (Non-binding guidance)
- [3] Commission Guidelines on prohibited AI practices, page 53 (Non-binding guidance)
- [4] Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (Artificial Intelligence Act), page 13 (Binding law)

## Q3. Is real-time facial recognition by police in public spaces allowed?
*Category: Prohibition · Expected:  · Time: 3.5 s · Best relevance: 5.61*

Yes – police may use real‑time facial‑recognition (remote biometric identification) in publicly accessible places, but only when it meets the narrow conditions set out in the AI Act.

**Key points**

* The AI Act (Article 5(1)(h)) permits real‑time remote biometric identification (RBI) by law‑enforcement in public spaces **only** if the use is strictly necessary to achieve a substantial public‑interest objective and the benefits outweigh the risks to fundamental rights[2].  
* The binding regulation (EU 2024/1689) adds that the system must be used **solely to confirm the identity of a specifically targeted individual** and must consider (a) the seriousness, probability and scale of the harm avoided, and (b) the impact on the rights and freedoms of all persons concerned[4].  
* Uses that do not fall under the listed objectives (e.g., identifying a shoplifter without a specific target) are prohibited[2].  

So, police can deploy real‑time facial recognition in public only for a clearly defined, targeted purpose that meets the strict necessity and proportionality tests laid out in the AI Act.

**Sources:**
- [1] Commission Guidelines on prohibited AI practices, page 130 (Non-binding guidance)
- [2] Commission Guidelines on prohibited AI practices, page 105 (Non-binding guidance)
- [3] Commission Guidelines on prohibited AI practices, page 111 (Non-binding guidance)
- [4] Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (Artificial Intelligence Act), page 15 (Binding law)

## Q4. What is the maximum fine for using a prohibited AI practice?
*Category: Penalties · Expected:  · Time: 2.6 s · Best relevance: 7.03*

The maximum fine for using a prohibited AI practice is up to **€ 35 million, or if the offender is an undertaking, up to 7 % of its total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year, whichever is higher**[4].  

The same ceiling is reflected in the Commission’s non‑binding guidance, which notes that violations of the prohibitions in Article 5 can be fined up to € 35 million or 7 % of worldwide turnover for undertakings[1].  

So, whether the breach is dealt with under the binding AI Act or the guidance, the top penalty is € 35 million or 7 % of global turnover, whichever is greater.

## Q5. Do I need to label deepfakes?
*Category: Transparency · Expected:  · Time: 4.3 s · Best relevance: 6.42*

Yes – under the AI Act you must label deep‑fake image, audio or video so that people can see it was artificially generated or manipulated, unless an exemption applies.

**Key points**

* **Legal requirement** – Article 50(4) first sub‑paragraph obliges any deployer of an AI system that creates or alters image, audio or video deep‑fakes to “disclose that the deep‑fake content has been artificially generated or manipulated” [2].  
* **How the label must work** – The Commission’s non‑binding guidance explains that the label must be clear, distinguishable and perceivable by natural persons (e.g., a visible or audible notice) and must not require users to use special tools [1].  
* **Exceptions** – The disclosure duty does not apply when the use is authorised by law for detecting, preventing, investigating or prosecuting a criminal offence, or when the content is part of an evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous work, in which case only a minimal disclosure of the existence of the generated content is required [2].  

So, in most commercial contexts you need to add an understandable label to any deep‑fake media you publish, unless one of the specific exemptions listed in the Act applies.

- [2] Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (Artificial Intelligence Act), page 59 (Binding law)
- [3] Commission Guidelines on the transparency obligations under Article 50 of the AI Act, page 44 (Non-binding guidance)

## Q6. What obligations do deployers of high-risk AI systems have?
*Category: Obligations · Expected:  · Time: 4.8 s · Best relevance: 7.47*

Deployers of high‑risk AI systems must (1) ensure the system is registered in the EU database and not use it if it is not [1]; (2) use the information supplied by the provider (Article 13) to carry out any required data‑protection impact assessment [1]; (3) assign competent human overseers with appropriate training, authority and support [3]; (4) guarantee that any input data they control is relevant and sufficiently representative for the system’s intended purpose [3]; (5) monitor the system’s operation continuously [3]; and (6) comply with any additional transparency duties toward natural persons, such as informing them that a high‑risk AI system is being used (Article 26(11) and Article 50) [4].  

These obligations are set out in the binding AI Act (Regulation EU 2024/1689) and are complemented by non‑binding Commission guidance on transparency.

## Q7. When did the prohibitions on AI practices start to apply?
*Category: Dates · Expected:  · Time: 3.1 s · Best relevance: 5.16*

The prohibitions on AI practices began to apply on **2 February 2025**[1].  

Key points  
- Article 113 of the AI Act sets 2 Feb 2025 as the entry‑into‑application date for the prohibitions in Article 5.  
- The bans apply to all AI systems, whether placed on the market before or after that date.  
- Although penalties and market‑surveillance rules only start on 2 August 2025, the prohibitions themselves are fully mandatory from 2 Feb 2025 and can be enforced in national courts.

## Q8. Does the AI Act apply to AI systems used only for military purposes?
*Category: Scope · Expected:  · Time: 3.3 s · Best relevance: 6.81*

The AI Act does **not** apply to AI systems that are placed on the market, put into service or used **exclusively** for military, defence or national‑security purposes; such systems fall outside the scope of the regulation. If the same system is also used for any civilian, humanitarian, law‑enforcement or public‑security purpose, then the AI Act does apply and the user must ensure compliance. [1]  

*Key points*  
- “Exclusively” is interpreted by Recital 24 of the AI Act: a system used only for military/defence/national‑security stays out of the Act’s scope.  
- The moment the system is used (temporarily or permanently) for any other purpose, it becomes subject to the AI Act and must meet its requirements.  
- This guidance is non‑binding interpretation from the European Commission; the binding rule is in the AI Act itself.  

So, purely military‑only AI systems are excluded, but any dual‑use or broader application brings the system within the AI Act.
